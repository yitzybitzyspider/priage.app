The ER waiting room is a blind spot.

In 2022, US emergency departments handled 155 million visits.[1] Many patients wait just to be triaged, a wait that often isn't even measured.[2] After triage, many wait again, sometimes for hours. Through both waits, it is hard for anyone to see who is getting worse.

PrIAge is working on ways to give ER nurses better information about patients while they wait. Nurses make the call. Right now we're talking with the people who do this work.

Some wait just to be triaged.

The wait before triage usually isn't counted in standard ER reporting. In one US emergency department, it grew sharply as the room got busier.[2]

1 in 5

of the sickest walk-in patients waited more than 15 minutes just to be triaged, longer than guidelines allow before they should see a doctor.

Canadian emergency department · Betz et al., CJEM, 2017 [7]

Some give up and leave before being seen.

When waits stretch, some people walk out. Across 10 US emergency departments, those who left unseen were more likely to come back, to need intensive care, and to die within 30 days than people who were seen and sent home.[4]

Admitted, and still waiting for a bed.

Patients who need a hospital bed often keep waiting in the emergency department. At one US hospital, the longer they waited, the more likely they were to die in the hospital.[5]

Almost 14,000

excess deaths in a single year were associated with emergency waits of 12 hours or longer, across one national health system.

England, 2023 · Royal College of Emergency Medicine, 2024 [6]

None of this is for lack of effort. ER nurses, doctors and hospital leaders work hard inside a system running past its limits: fewer beds, fewer staff, and patients who keep coming.

Fewer beds, and they're fuller.

Since the pandemic, US hospitals have been able to staff far fewer beds, while about as many patients need one each day. When a hospital passes 85% full, admitted patients wait in the ER for hours, however hard everyone works to free a bed.[8][9]

88.9%

of the months a hospital ran above 85% full, admitted patients waited in its ER longer than the 4-hour standard.

US hospitals, 2020 to 2021 · Janke et al., JAMA Network Open, 2022 [9]

The people watching the room are stretched thin.

ER nurses keep an eye on the waiting room while caring for everyone already inside. Nearly a third of US nurses who left a job named burnout as a reason, and most of those said there weren't enough staff.[10]

17.6%

of hospital nurses leave in a year, with emergency nurses among those leaving most often.

US hospitals, 2025 · NSI National Health Care Retention & RN Staffing Report, 2026 [11]

The strain shows up on the bill.

At one US stroke center, a patient waiting in the ER for a bed cost more each day than the same patient upstairs, and got fewer minutes of nursing time. Each nurse who leaves costs a hospital tens of thousands of dollars to replace.[12][11]

$60,090

to replace one bedside nurse who leaves. The average hospital loses $4.2 million to $6.2 million this way.

US hospitals, 2025 · NSI, 2026 [11]

$17 million

in extra hospital costs in one year, along with 300 extra deaths, tied to the most crowded ER days in one state.

California, 187 hospitals · Sun et al., Annals of Emergency Medicine, 2013 [13]
